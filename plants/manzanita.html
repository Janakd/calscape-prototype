Overview

Manzanita is an evergreen shrub native to California's chaparral and coastal scrub. Its smooth, mahogany-red bark, blue-green leaves, and clusters of small urn-shaped flowers in late winter make it one of the most distinctive plants of the California landscape. 'Howard McMinn' is a garden-friendly hybrid valued for its compact, mounded form and superior performance in cultivated settings compared to most wild species.

Manzanitas grow slowly and live long — wild specimens can reach 50+ years. In a garden, they form a sculptural anchor: a single mature plant can carry an entire bed.

Habitat & Range

Wild Manzanita species are found across California's chaparral, coastal scrub, and lower-elevation forests, from Baja California north into Oregon. They thrive in lean, well-drained, often rocky soils on slopes where water moves quickly.

'Howard McMinn' is a hybrid (A. densiflora × A. uva-ursi) developed at the University of California Botanical Garden in the 1950s — bred for garden adaptability.

Garden Use

Excellent as a sculptural anchor in a dry garden, low-water hedge, or slope stabilizer. The smooth red bark provides visual interest year-round, especially in winter when most other plants are dormant.

Companion plants: California buckwheat, sticky monkeyflower, deergrass, ceanothus, salvias. All share Manzanita's preference for lean soil and minimal summer water.

Growing Conditions

  • Sun: Full sun. Tolerates light afternoon shade inland.
  • Water: Very low once established. Summer water is the single most common cause of decline; do not irrigate established plants.
  • Soil: Lean, well-drained, slightly acidic preferred. Tolerates clay if planted on a slope or mound for drainage.
  • USDA Zone: 7–10.
  • Establishment: Water deeply once a week the first summer; taper off in year 2; by year 3, rely on winter rain only.

Propagation

Manzanita is famously challenging to propagate. Three methods are viable, with very different effort and success rates. Cuttings are most common in nurseries; layering is the highest-success method for home gardeners; seed is mostly for research and patience-rich enthusiasts.

From seed

Difficulty: High · Timing: Sow in fall
  1. Collect ripe berries in fall; macerate in water to separate seeds.
  2. Treat to simulate fire: pour boiling water over seeds and let stand 24 hours, or use commercial smoke water.
  3. Cold-stratify: refrigerate moist seeds at 35–40°F for 30–60 days.
  4. Sow in a well-drained gritty mix; keep slightly moist.
  5. Germination is erratic over 1–3 years; expect 1–2% success even with full treatment.

From cuttings

Difficulty: Medium · Timing: Late summer to early fall
  1. Take 4–6 inch semi-hardwood cuttings from current-year growth.
  2. Strip the lower leaves; cut just below a node.
  3. Dip in 0.8% IBA rooting hormone (higher for stubborn species).
  4. Insert into a fast-draining mix: 50/50 perlite and coarse sand.
  5. Provide humidity (dome or mist) and bottom heat (~70°F).
  6. Roots form in 6–12 weeks for 'Howard McMinn'; some wild species take 6+ months.

Layering

Difficulty: Low · Timing: Late winter to early spring
  1. Select a low, flexible branch from a healthy parent plant.
  2. Wound the underside of the branch where it will contact soil.
  3. Bury that section under 3–4 inches of soil; pin with a stone or wire.
  4. Keep moist (the parent plant will do most of the work).
  5. Roots form over 1–2 years.
  6. Cut the new plant from the parent once well-rooted; transplant in fall.

Step-by-step: taking a cutting

A visual timeline of the cuttings workflow, from collection to rooting.

  1. Summer
    Take 3–6 inch semi-hardwood cuttings from healthy, non-flowering shoots.
  2. Immediately after cutting
    Remove the lower leaves and trim the stem just below a node.
  3. Before inserting
    Dip the cut end in 0.8% IBA rooting hormone powder or gel to encourage root formation.
  4. Same day
    Insert the cutting into a damp, porous, sandy rooting medium (50/50 perlite + coarse sand).
  5. 4–8 weeks
    Place under mist or cover with a humidity dome; keep in indirect light. Bottom heat at ~70°F speeds rooting.
  6. 6–12 weeks total
    Tug-test for resistance. Once rooted, harden off over 1–2 weeks before transplanting.

Cleaning the seed

Two approaches for cleaning and storing seed after collection — pick whichever fits your setup.

Home

Home cleaning method

A simplified version of seed cleaning suitable for home propagators.

  1. Lay seeds on a clean surface to dry for 7–10 days.
  2. Gently rub the dried berries between your fingers to separate seeds from the dry pulp.
  3. Pour the mixture through a kitchen sieve to remove debris and lighter chaff.
  4. Store seeds in a paper envelope in a cool, dry, dark place until you're ready to stratify.
Pro

Professional cleaning method

The technique used by restoration nurseries to fully remove seed from husk or capsule before storage.

  1. Run dried seed through a seed cleaner or graduated laboratory sieve set.
  2. Apply controlled airflow (column blower) to remove lightweight chaff.
  3. Treat with a mild fungicide if storing for more than one season.
  4. Store in a humidity- and temperature-controlled environment: 5°C / 30% RH is standard.

— A volunteer restoration grower

Glossary

Key terms used across the propagation literature.

Cold Stratification /ˌstræt·ɪ·fɪˈkeɪ·ʃən/
Exposing seeds to cold, moist conditions to simulate winter dormancy. Required by many native plants before they will germinate in spring.
Scarification /ˌskær·ɪ·fɪˈkeɪ·ʃən/
Physically or chemically breaking down a hard seed coat so water and oxygen can reach the embryo and trigger germination. For manzanita, near-boiling water for 24 hours is the standard scarification.
Pre-germination Treatment
Any process applied to seeds before sowing to improve germination rates. Includes scarification, stratification, smoke treatment, and gibberellic acid soaks.
Days Cold Stratified
The number of days seeds need to be kept cold and moist before they are ready to germinate. Manzanita: 30–60 days at 35–40°F.
Hormone PPM
The concentration of rooting hormone, measured in parts per million, used to stimulate root development in cuttings. Manzanita typically responds best to 0.8% IBA (8,000 ppm).
Disinfectant
A chemical agent applied to cuttings or tools to prevent fungal and bacterial infection during rooting.
Growing Media & Preparation
The soil or substrate mix used to grow plants, and how it's prepared before use. For manzanita cuttings: 50/50 perlite + coarse sand, sterile, well-draining.
Harden Off
Gradually acclimatize a plant to outdoor conditions — temperature swings, direct sun, and wind — before permanent planting. Usually done over 1–2 weeks.
Cleaning Method — Home
A simplified version of seed cleaning suitable for home propagators: hand-rub berries, sieve, store dry.
Cleaning Method — Pro
The professional technique used to remove seed from its husk or capsule before storage or sowing, using mechanical sieves and controlled airflow.

Wildlife Value

  • Pollinators: Manzanita's winter-blooming urn-shaped flowers are a critical nectar source for native bumblebees and honeybees when little else is in bloom.
  • Birds: Berries feed quail, towhees, mockingbirds, and many migratory species in late spring and summer.
  • Larval host: Several Arctostaphylos-specialist moth species require manzanita as host plants.
